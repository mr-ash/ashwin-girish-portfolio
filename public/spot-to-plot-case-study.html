Case study · Community-centred service design

Spot-to-Plot.
Turning neglected corners into community-owned spaces

A lightweight coordination tool that helps residents of The Liberties, Dublin, spot overlooked micro-plots, gather ideas together, and connect with student volunteers to transform them. I carried this project through the full ARRIVE process — from field research on Liberties streets to a community-tested, refined concept.

01 · My role & contributions

What I owned on this project

The project began as team research and became individual design work from the Reframe phase onward. Across both stages, I owned the thread that became Spot-to-Plot — from first field observations to the validated, refined concept.

The brief: empower residents of The Liberties to transform overlooked micro-plots — unused patches, vacant corners, unsafe spots — into ecological and social green spaces. Our research surfaced the deeper problem: residents face systemic neglect from institutions and external stigma, so the project had to place resident agency at its centre.

What I did — at a glance
  • Field research: attended Liberties tours, observed and documented the area through notes, photos and video
  • Topic guide: formulated the interview topic guide used for resident interviews
  • Analysis: sorted interview data, ran affinity mapping, authored 4 insight notes
  • Persona: built and visualised "Marie" from real resident quotes
  • Ideation: ran Think-with-Things, wrote 5 HMWs, developed 4 concept directions
  • Concept: defined the full Spot-to-Plot feature set and flows
  • Validation: presented to Liberties residents, designed the conversation prompts, interpreted feedback
  • Refinement: pivoted the concept, scored it against community values, wrote implementation scenarios
02 · Audit & Research

On the ground in The Liberties

What I did

I joined the Liberties walking tours and treated them as field research — observing how people used (and avoided) spaces, taking notes, and capturing photos and video of candidate micro-plots. I explored spaces around the neighbourhood that could connect to our brief, and before meeting anyone I completed a positionality exercise — "interviewing" a public building (an Intreo office) — to confront how my Computer Science instinct to "fix things efficiently" could bias what I'd see and hear.

I then formulated the topic guide for our qualitative interviews — structuring the core topics broadly enough to surface hidden aspects of residents' lives rather than just confirming our assumptions — and took part in the interviews themselves.

What I found

The data broke my starting assumption. I had arrived expecting to design "pretty little micro-plots to fill gaps in the green infrastructure." Instead, residents were already rolling up their sleeves — fundraising, running coffee mornings, even urban beekeeping — with zero institutional support. They had a sophisticated ecological vision of their own: rainwater harvesting, composting, beehives, enclosed gardens. The wall they kept hitting wasn't a lack of ideas. It was institutions that wouldn't listen.

03 · Reframe — sorting the data

Four insight notes I authored from the interviews

Working from the interview transcripts, I clustered quotes through affinity mapping and distilled them into four insight notes — each one grounded in residents' actual words.

Insight 01

Resilience Through Belonging

Long-term residents are deeply emotionally attached to The Liberties and strongly opposed to displacement. Informal care networks act as a safety net.

"I've lived here 57 years and I'm not leaving."

"We look out for one another."

Insight 02

Grassroots Leadership

Residents don't wait for institutions — they self-organise, lobby and fundraise, creating real-world change despite inadequate resources.

"We do it ourselves because no one else will."

"Neighbours organise on Facebook."

Insight 03

Local Care Networks

Allotments, memory trees and coffee mornings offer vital mental, social and nutritional support — low-barrier ways to connect and heal.

"We fundraise every year for the children's holiday."

Insight 04

Enduring Systemic Neglect

Decades of unfulfilled needs and broken promises have destroyed confidence in institutions, leaving the community to fill structural voids.

"The council just offered to move me instead of fixing what's wrong."

I synthesised Marie directly from the interview quotes and insight clusters: a resourceful, determined long-term resident who leads community projects while battling repair delays, unsafe communal spaces and the feeling of being unheard. I wrote her POV statement — maintaining local culture and advocating for safe green spaces so future generations inherit the same belonging — and it became the test every later design decision had to pass. To bring her world to life, I also crafted an AI image prompt depicting her community's two realities: the warm, connected side and the neglected, shadowed side.

04 · Ideation

From tangible play to five opportunity questions

I started with Think-with-Things — using tangible objects to make the community's problems and opportunities physical and playful instead of abstract. From there I reframed my insights into five How-Might-We questions:

HMW 01

…make spotting and pinning a neglected micro-plot feel exciting and empowering instead of like complaining?

HMW 02

…tap into nearby student skills, local businesses and children's ideas as co-designers inside each micro-plot?

HMW 03

…make small improvements visible to everyone, so residents feel growing pride and ownership?

HMW 04

…give residents a low-risk way to act on unsafe micro-plots without waiting on slow official channels?

HMW 05

…help residents with energy but no skills, and those with skills but no time, find each other?

Four directions I developed — and why I chose the fourth

Ideation 1

Bridgefoot Park beautification

A direct physical intervention: beautifying Bridge Foot Street Park as a flagship transformed space.

Ideation 2

Terrace conversation lanterns

Ambient street objects sparking neighbourly conversation and shared ownership of terrace spaces.

Ideation 3

Reward drink stations

Incentive points rewarding residents for caring for shared spaces with small local perks.

Ideation 4

Digital plot map

A web map where residents pin plots, discuss and rally help — the only direction that scaled across every micro-plot and answered all five HMWs.

The first three ideas each fixed one space or one motivation. The map concept was different: it didn't do the transforming — it connected the people who already wanted to. That matched what my insights kept saying: the community has ideas and energy; what's missing is a shared structure.

05 · The concept I designed

Spot-to-Plot: a map, a conversation, a call for hands

I deliberately specified this as a web map with a simple mobile-friendly form — not a full native app — to keep the barrier to entry as low as possible.

📍 Spot it

Residents upload a photo and address, tag the plot — unused / unsafe / messy / potential — and add 1–2 preferred futures, like "bee bed + small seat".

💬 Shape it

A public map shows every spot with colour-coded status pins — red = identified, yellow = ideas forming, green = in progress or cared for — each with its own comment thread, like a mini forum.

🤝 Transform it

A one-tap "volunteer request" emails a curated contact list — student unions and local NGOs — with a link to that plot. Optional AI visualisations help residents picture the future garden or seating.

06 · Validate — testing my assumptions

I took the concept back to the community

How I ran the session

I presented Spot-to-Plot at a community session in The Liberties, walking residents through the key screens and using Bridgefoot Park as a live scenario — showing how a plot would move from "spotted" to "discussed" to "transformed". Rather than asking "do you like it?", I designed open conversation prompts: "Would you use something like this?" and "How would you usually get people together for a micro-plot project?" — so residents could compare the concept against their real organising habits, not react to UI polish.

"I'm not really into technology."

— Liberties resident, validation session

What I observed & interpreted

  • One resident's low digital confidence flagged an age-and-habit barrier my phone-first concept had ignored.
  • Residents kept steering the conversation back to council neglect, slow repairs and unsafe spaces — not features. I read this as the real signal: a deeper need to be heard and recognised, not just organised.
  • The session became a space for residents to re-voice their struggles. I concluded that validation isn't "do they like my app?" — it's "does this honour how they already self-organise and advocate?"

How I responded: three refined intentions

Instead of discarding the concept, I re-architected it as a lighter coordination layer that works alongside Facebook groups, word-of-mouth and coffee mornings.

Refined intention 1

Visible & discussable

I kept the shared map but added alternative input channels — a neighbour or volunteer can log a plot on behalf of non-app users ("identified by / logged by"), and plots can sync with existing Facebook advocacy.

Refined intention 2

Resident-led, not top-down

I reframed the chat rooms and statuses as tools for residents to coordinate what they're already doing — not an external project-management system imposed on them.

Refined intention 3

Low entry barrier

I dropped the native-app ambition for a simple web version plus offline hooks I proposed myself: posters, printed maps, and a QR-code board in the local community centre.

Scoring both versions against community values

I built a value matrix to check my pivot against what residents actually care about — community values, movement goals, and emancipation criteria. The refined version improved on every single dimension.

07 · Execute & emancipation

Designing for ownership, not dependency

In the final phase I articulated why this direction matters: residents act as initiators and owners — they decide what to transform and when a plot counts as "Completed". The tool doesn't replace political responsibility; it gives residents a way to document evidence, priorities and action without waiting for the council to move first. I wrote two implementation scenarios to make that concrete:

Scenario · Marie & Oliver Bond

Marie points out an unsafe corner near her home. A neighbour logs the plot on her behalf, prints a small QR-code poster for the community centre, and over weeks the chat fills with suggestions. Student volunteers join, and the space gains a bench and planting.

Scenario · Teens at Bridgefoot Park

Teenagers log a dark, messy corner that feels unsafe at night. Neighbours and a youth worker add ideas in the chat and at a youth session, agreeing on planting and better lighting — turning the spot into a cared-for space.

The role flip that defined this project

At the start, I thought my job was to design neat little micro-plots to brighten gaps in the area. Spending time with residents flipped that completely: they already had strong ideas and were organising themselves — the blockage was institutions not listening. My work shifted from "what can I build for them?" to "how can I create ways for them to lead and be heard?" — from designer as problem-solver to designer as someone who clears a path for community power.

If I continued this work

  • Co-design non-digital touchpoints with residents — physical boards and street-level plot markers.
  • Run participatory mapping sessions where residents decide which features truly help them.
  • Explore how ownership and maintenance stay within the community, with students and designers in supporting roles.
